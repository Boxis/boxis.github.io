## The Book

**Title:** _The Song of Achilles_
**Author:** Madeline Miller
**Finished:** August 20, 2026
**Rating:** ★★★★½

## One-paragraph summary

A retelling of the Trojan War myth, narrated by Patroclus — a minor, mostly voiceless character in Homer's _Iliad_ — from the moment he's exiled to the court of King Peleus, where he meets Peleus's son, the prophesied greatest warrior of his generation, Achilles. The novel follows their friendship as it deepens through years of training under the centaur Chiron, into a devoted relationship that runs straight through the Greek campaign against Troy. Miller keeps the mythic scale of the source material but tells it at a human, intimate register: less about the clash of armies and more about two people bound together as Achilles is pulled toward the glory (and cost) fate has laid out for him.

## Themes and what stood out

- **Giving voice to the margins of the myth.** Patroclus is barely a presence in the _Iliad_; centering him — and making explicit the relationship with Achilles that classical sources leave ambiguous — reframes a story usually told at epic distance as something much more personal.
- **Glory versus a quiet life.** Achilles is caught between _kleos_ (eternal fame through a short, glorious life at Troy) and an ordinary, long one — the novel makes that trade-off the emotional center of the book rather than background prophecy.
- **Coming of age as a pair.** The early chapters on Mount Pelion — training, boyhood, identity forming alongside someone else's — do a lot of the work that makes the later war chapters land.
- **The cost of war on those who aren't heroes.** Characters like Briseis surface the human toll that epic mythology usually flattens in favor of its central warriors.
- **Prose that stays lyrical without losing momentum.** Miller's writing carries the tactile, sensory feel of myth while still reading like a fast, character-driven novel.

## Who should read it

Strong pick for anyone who likes mythology retellings that add interiority the originals don't bother with — especially if you've read (or plan to read) Miller's _Circe_, which does something similar for a different figure from the same mythic world. Also a good fit for readers who want a love story embedded in a coming-of-age arc rather than treated as a subplot. Worth going in aware that it follows the emotional shape of Greek tragedy — the source material doesn't end lightly.
