## The Book

**Title:** _Boy's Life_
**Author:** Robert McCammon
**Finished:** June 17, 2026
**Rating:** ★★★★½

## One-paragraph summary

Narrated by an adult Cory Mackenson looking back on the summer he turned twelve, the novel opens with Cory and his father witnessing a car plunge into a lake with a murdered man handcuffed to the wheel — a mystery that runs quietly under everything else that happens that year in the small fictional town of Zephyr, Alabama, in 1964. Around that central thread, McCammon builds out a whole town of local legend and folklore — half-real, half-imagined — while following Cory's coming of age, his bond with his father, and his own growing identity as a storyteller. It reads less like a straight mystery and more like a nostalgic, occasionally magical-realist portrait of childhood with a murder running underneath it.

## Themes and what stood out

- **Genre blending.** A murder mystery, a coming-of-age story, and magical realism sit comfortably in the same book — the mystery gives it forward momentum, but it's not really what the novel is about.
- **Zephyr as its own character.** The town is populated with local legend — a river monster, a folk healer, ghostly figures — treated with the same matter-of-fact belief a child would give them, which blurs the line between what's real and imagined throughout.
- **The loss of a child's way of seeing.** There's a persistent, elegiac undertone about growing up and gradually losing the ability to see the world with wonder — the adult narrator's perspective makes this ache more than a straight childhood narrative would.
- **The father-son relationship.** Cory's bond with his father, especially as his father is shaken by what they witnessed, is the emotional backbone of the book more than the mystery plot itself.
- **Race and division in the segregated South.** Seen through a child's slowly sharpening awareness rather than delivered as commentary, which makes it land more than it might otherwise.
- **Storytelling as its own subject.** Cory's development as a budding writer threads through the book — it's as much about why we tell stories as it is a story itself.

## Favorite quote

> No one ever grows up. They may look grown-up, but it's just the clay of time. Men and women are still children deep in their hearts.

## Who should read it

A natural fit if you liked the nostalgic, small-town coming-of-age register of Stephen King's _The Body_ (the novella behind _Stand By Me_) or Ray Bradbury's _Dandelion Wine_ — or the Southern-childhood-with-a-dark-undercurrent feel of _To Kill a Mockingbird_. Best for readers who don't need a mystery to move at a thriller's pace; this one is patient, atmospheric, and character-driven, with the murder more of a thread than the point.
